# Agroforestry and Intercropping as Biodiversity Interventions

Agroforestry, the deliberate integration of trees with crops or livestock, is one of the few interventions that simultaneously improves soil health, climate resilience, and biodiversity outcomes on the same plot of land without removing it from agricultural production.

The mechanism operates on several pathways at once: tree canopy reduces soil surface temperature and evaporation, which increases soil moisture retention; leaf litter and root turnover add organic matter, raising soil organic carbon over multi-year timescales; and the added vertical and structural complexity creates microhabitats that support insects, birds, and other taxa absent from single-layer monoculture fields.

Meta-analyses across tropical and semi-arid systems report fragmentation index reductions of roughly 20-30% over 3-5 years following conversion from monoculture to agroforestry or intercropping, alongside soil moisture retention gains of 10-15%. Because these effects compound over several growing seasons, agroforestry is best classified as a long-term intervention, distinct from short-term interventions such as pollinator strips that can show measurable effect within a single season.

Source basis: Torralba et al., Agroforestry and biodiversity: a meta-analysis, Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment (2016); FAO, Conservation Agriculture and Soil Carbon Sequestration Report (2017).
