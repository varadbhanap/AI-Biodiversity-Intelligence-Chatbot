# Soil Organic Carbon and Biodiversity

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is widely regarded as the best single indicator of overall soil health because it drives microbial biomass, water holding capacity, and nutrient cycling simultaneously. FAO's global soil assessments classify SOC below 0.5% as critically degraded and above 2% as biologically healthy for most cultivated soils.

In semi-arid and degraded agricultural land, SOC decline is frequently caused by continuous monocropping, residue removal, and excessive tillage. Restoring SOC through legume cover cropping, reduced tillage, or organic amendments has been shown in multiple long-term trials to increase microbial diversity within a single growing season, with measurable increases in soil organic carbon (roughly 15-25%) accruing over 2-3 years.

SOC also interacts with moisture: each 1% increase in SOC can increase soil water holding capacity meaningfully, which in turn supports vegetation cover and the habitat base for aboveground species richness. This makes SOC a high-leverage entry point for biodiversity interventions, since it improves both a below-ground (microbial) and above-ground (vegetation, habitat) pathway at once.

Source basis: FAO Global Soil Partnership, Status of the World's Soil Resources (2015); FAO Conservation Agriculture and Soil Carbon Sequestration Report (2017); Lal, Soil carbon sequestration impacts on global climate change and food security, Science (2004).
