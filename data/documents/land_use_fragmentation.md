# Land Use, Habitat Fragmentation, and Species Richness

Land use structure is one of the strongest predictors of local terrestrial biodiversity loss globally. Comparative studies across biomes consistently find that converting natural or mixed-use land to intensive monoculture reduces local species richness substantially relative to natural habitat baselines, with the effect compounding as fragmentation increases.

Fragmentation matters independently of total habitat area: breaking a landscape into smaller, disconnected patches isolates populations, reduces effective breeding ranges, and increases edge effects (exposure to wind, pests, and human disturbance at patch boundaries). Long-term fragmentation experiments have shown that patch isolation reduces both species richness and genetic diversity over time, and that these effects can persist for decades even after fragmentation stops.

Agroforestry and intercropping are the most consistently effective land-use interventions for reversing this trend on working agricultural land, because they add structural and vertical complexity without removing the land from production. Meta-analyses of agroforestry systems report species richness gains of up to 60% relative to adjacent monocultures, alongside improved soil moisture retention from canopy shading.

Source basis: Newbold et al., Global effects of land use on local terrestrial biodiversity, Nature (2015); Haddad et al., Habitat fragmentation and its lasting impact on Earth's ecosystems, Science Advances (2015); Torralba et al., Agroforestry and biodiversity: a meta-analysis, Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment (2016).
